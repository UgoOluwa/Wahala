WAHALA
Track 3 - Safety, Reporting and Protection
Ugochukwu Odunukwe

Live: https://wahala-beta.vercel.app  (judges can skip the disguise: /?open=1)
Response desk: https://wahala-beta.vercel.app/responder
Code: https://github.com/UgoOluwa/Wahala

A prototype for judging. It does not dispatch real help. In a real emergency,
call 112.


WHAT I BUILT AND WHY

Panic apps are loud. They sit on your homescreen with their own name and icon,
they buzz, and they light up the screen to tell you help is on the way. That
works for someone taken on a road in Zamfara. It is dangerous for a woman being
beaten in Lagos, because her husband is holding the phone. For her the
notification is not the alert. It is what gets her hurt.

So I built Wahala silent by default. It opens as a calculator that actually
calculates. You type 112 and press equals to get in. There is no account and no
phone number. Nothing ever rings, buzzes or shows a badge, not even when the
agency replies. One tap on Leave now erases everything and puts the calculator
back.

Danger has two speeds, so there are two ways in. If you are in danger now, it
is one button and no typing, with GPS already running before you press. If you
are reporting abuse, you can type what happened and who did it, and hold back
your location if being found would make things worse.

Getting the report out was the hard part. GPS works without internet because it
uses satellites, so the coordinates always exist and only the sending has to
wait. Wahala tries three things in order: send it, or open a prefilled SMS if
there is signal but no data, or hold it on the phone and send it by itself when
a bar comes back. The whole report fits in 43 characters so one SMS carries it.

Reports go to a named body, and you see which one before you send. Sexual abuse
goes to Mirabel Centre, WARIF and Lagos DSVA. Kidnapping goes to 112, NSCDC and
NAPTIP. The desk replies with an ETA, and that reply arrives silently in your
own language, because a reply you cannot read is not help.

Then two things most tools miss. Every report gives you a four-digit callback
code. Anyone claiming to be a responder must say it first, because "we got your
report, come outside" is how people get lured out, and in an abuse case the
person most likely to send that is the one you reported. And while you wait,
the app tells you three concrete things to do for your situation, offline, in
all five languages. You can also save trusted people, kept only on your phone,
and reach all of them with one SMS. For kidnapping there is a dead-man's switch
that fires in ten minutes unless you cancel. It is registered on the server the
moment you arm it, so it still goes out if the phone is taken or dies.


SOURCES, AND WHAT I DID NOT DO

One day, so no interviews and no fieldwork. I used no dataset and I quote no
statistics anywhere, in the app or here, because I could not verify a figure
properly in the time and I would rather have none than one I had not checked.

The organisations are real and publicly known, but I did not contact any of
them and none has reviewed this. 112 is the only number I was sure enough to
publish. Every other hotline is blank and labelled unverified in the app
itself. The safety advice follows what support services publish widely, but no
clinician has checked it. On design I looked at Aspire News for the disguise
idea, and Noonlight, Bright Sky and Hollie Guard for how they handle dispatch.
I took no code or wording from them.


TRUST AND ACCURACY

Real and working: capture, GPS, the offline queue, five languages, the
disguise, quick exit, the callback code, trusted contacts, the dead-man's
switch. Simulated but built and in the repo: the response desk. Not connected:
real dispatch, because no public API exists for it.

Where it cannot do something it says so. A web app cannot send SMS silently, so
you press send yourself. Cancelling the dead-man's switch after it fires is
refused instead of quietly accepted, because responders may already be moving.
A typed reply that cannot be translated arrives labelled as English. The README
lists the gaps, including one my own feature created: the desk is not behind a
login yet, so in this prototype anyone opening it could see a callback code.


USE OF AI TOOLS

The idea and the product decisions are mine, including the one this is all
built on. I wrote the code with Claude Code as a pair, and it drafted this
summary and the deck. The translations are AI-drafted and need a native speaker
before anyone relies on them, which is the biggest caveat in the project. I did
not clean up the commit history.


WHAT IS NEXT

Verified hotlines are phone calls, not code. A real desk needs one pilot
partner behind a login. The translations need a native speaker. USSD is the
only real build left, and it matters most, because the people furthest from
help have the cheapest phones. None of it is a rewrite. What is already decided
is the part I care about: that this thing stays quiet even when the news is
good.
